=== Answers ===

1. None of the targeted usernames (admin, root, backup, oracle) are current
   accounts. All current accounts (alice, bob, eve, david) had 0 failed logins.
   This suggests the attacker did not know the real user list and was guessing
   common, default or generic account names from a wordlist.

2. admin, root and backup are LEGACY accounts found only in the old backup list.
   If they still existed, root would be the most dangerous because it has full
   control of the system. admin is also dangerous because it is probably a
   privileged account, and it received the most attempts (17, HIGH risk).

3. No. The disabled account david had 0 accepted logins, and there were no
   accepted logins from any external (non-192.168.) address. This suggests the
   password-guessing attack against SSH did not succeed in this log. It does NOT
   prove the system was not compromised: the attacker could have used other
   methods (e.g. stolen credentials from an internal machine, SSH keys, another
   service), and the log may be incomplete or could have been tampered with.

4. alice and bob both have plaintext credentials in passwords.txt and successful
   logins. alice logged in from 4 different internal addresses
   (192.168.1.55, 192.168.1.35, 192.168.1.40, 192.168.1.20).
   bob logged in from 3 different internal address(es) (192.168.1.20 192.168.1.35 192.168.1.40 ).
   This combination is worth reporting because anyone who read the passwords
   file could log in as these users, and logins from several machines may mean
   the credentials are being used by someone other than the owner (lateral
   movement). Their passwords should be reset and the file removed.
   Note: alice and bob logged in from three of the same machines
   (192.168.1.20, 192.168.1.35, 192.168.1.40). Two different accounts being
   used from the same hosts supports the idea that one person is using both
   sets of credentials from passwords.txt.
